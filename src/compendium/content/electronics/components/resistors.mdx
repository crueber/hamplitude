## What it is

A resistor opposes current, turning electrical energy into heat. It sets bias currents, drops voltages, divides signals, limits current through an LED and, in larger form, soaks up power in a <Ref to="dummy-loads">dummy load</Ref>. Its value is in ohms (Ω), following <Ref to="ohms-law" />.

## Reading the colour code

Small resistors are too tiny for printed numbers, so they wear bands. The first bands are digits, the next is a multiplier (how many zeros), and the last is tolerance. Precision parts use a third digit band. Common tolerance bands are brown ±1%, red ±2%, gold ±5% and silver ±10%.

| green | 5 | silver | ×0.01 (multiplier), ±10% |

Surface-mount resistors print numbers instead: **472** means 47 followed by two zeros, 4700 Ω or 4.7 kΩ.

## Choosing one

- **Value.** Resistors come in standard steps, so you pick the nearest. The common E12 series (typically ±10% parts) runs 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82, then repeats ×10. The E24 series (typically ±5%) adds the values in between.
- **Power.** The resistor turns I² × R into heat. A 100 Ω resistor across 12 V dissipates 12² ÷ 100 = 1.44 W, so a 1/4 W part would burn. Choose a rating at least twice the calculated dissipation, and expect a hot resistor to be too hot to touch.
- **Construction.** Carbon film and metal film are the everyday types, metal film being tighter and quieter. Wire-wound resistors handle high power but are coils, so they have inductance and are poor at RF. Specially wound non-inductive power resistors are used where RF matters.

## Variable resistors

A potentiometer has three terminals: the two ends of a resistive track and a moving wiper. Used as a volume control, the wiper picks off a fraction of the voltage. Wired as a two-terminal rheostat, it simply varies the resistance.
