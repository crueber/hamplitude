## What it is

A **radiogram** is a written message in a standard format, so that many operators can pass it along a chain of stations and it arrives unchanged. The format is essentially the same whether it travels by voice, Morse, packet or Winlink. Handling it well is mostly discipline: **copy it exactly and pass it exactly**.

## The parts

<Radiograms_Anatomy />

A radiogram has four parts, in this order:

1. **Preamble:** the information needed to **track** the message. It holds the message number, the **precedence**, the station of origin, the **check**, the place of origin and the date. Optional **handling instructions** can also appear.
2. **Address:** who the message is for, with a telephone number if possible.
3. **Text:** the message itself, kept short.
4. **Signature:** who it is from.

The **check** is the **number of words** in the text, so a receiving station can tell whether anything was lost or added. Count only the text, not the address or signature. The example's text has 10 words, so the check is 10.

## Precedence

| Precedence | Abbreviation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency | EMERGENCY | life-and-death urgency |
| Priority | P | important, time-sensitive, but not an emergency |
| Welfare | W | inquiring about or reporting the health and welfare of a person in an affected area |
| Routine | R | everything else |

Handling follows the precedence: emergency and priority messages go first.

## Sending it

- **Do not change the text.** The sender's words are the message, even if you would phrase it differently.
- **Keep text short and plain.** Radiograms are meant to be brief, with no unusual abbreviations.
- On voice, pass each part clearly and slowly, with a pause or spoken break between parts, and spell unusual words with the <Ref to="phonetic-alphabet">phonetic alphabet</Ref>.
- The receiving station should **repeat the check** and confirm before the sender moves on.

<Callout kind="note" title="Content still matters">Part 97 forbids obscuring the meaning of a message and bars certain content, such as business traffic. Send only what the person who wrote it wanted sent, and do not release it to anyone else.</Callout>

## Where radiograms are used

National and local **message-handling networks** relay them; see <Ref to="nts-and-mars" />. During a disaster, health-and-welfare traffic is the common use: people outside the area asking if family are safe. The same fields appear in simplified form on agency forms, such as the ICS-213 described in <Ref to="incident-command-system" />.
