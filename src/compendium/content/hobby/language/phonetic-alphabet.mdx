## Why spell with words

Many letters sound alike over a noisy radio link: B, D, P and T; M and N; F and S. The phonetic alphabet replaces each letter with a distinct word, so "W5YI" is copied correctly as **Whiskey Five Yankee India**. Use it for call signs, names, places and anything unusual.

## The alphabet

The words were chosen to be easy to say and hard to confuse in many languages and on weak signals. That is why the official spellings are **Alfa** and **Juliett**: spelled that way, speakers of any language pronounce them as intended. English-speaking hams usually say "Alpha" and "Juliet", and nobody minds.

## When and how to use it

- **Stick to the standard words.** Improvised words ("Washington for W") can be mistaken for something else. Everyone listening knows the standard ones.
- **Match the effort to the signal.** On a clear local repeater, saying your call plainly is fine. In a pile-up or on a weak HF path, spell it every time.
- **Say it at a steady pace**, one word per character, without running them together.
- **Spell only what is needed.** A name or a hard-to-hear part of a call may be enough.
- **Numbers**: say them digit by digit ("one four two"), not as "one hundred forty-two".
- **Use it for identification.** In the US, Part 97 encourages a standard phonetic alphabet as an aid to correct station identification on voice.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Learn it by using it">Spell the license plates and street signs you pass. After a week the words come without thought, and in an emergency or net they are what keeps messages accurate. It is also the standard way to spell difficult words when handling messages.</Callout>
