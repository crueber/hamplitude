## Three letters, a whole sentence

Q-codes are three-letter signals that all start with Q. They were created for telegraph operators so that common messages could be sent in a few characters, in any language. Each code has two forms: a **statement**, and the same code with a question mark, which turns it into a **question**.

## The common ones

These are the Q-codes an operator meets every week. The official list is much longer, and only a fraction of it is used in ham radio.

| Code | As a statement | As a question |
|---|---|---|
| **QRL** | This frequency is busy | Is this frequency in use? |
| **QRM** | I am being interfered with by other stations | Are you being interfered with? |
| **QRN** | I am troubled by static (natural noise) | Are you troubled by static? |
| **QRO** | Increase power | Shall I increase power? |
| **QRP** | Reduce power | Shall I reduce power? |
| **QRQ** | Send faster | Shall I send faster? |
| **QRS** | Send slower | Shall I send slower? |
| **QRT** | I am stopping transmission (shutting down) | Shall I stop sending? |
| **QRU** | I have nothing for you | Have you anything for me? |
| **QRV** | I am ready | Are you ready? |
| **QRX** | I will call you again later; please stand by | When will you call again? |
| **QRZ** | You are being called by ... | Who is calling me? |
| **QSB** | Your signals are fading | Are my signals fading? |
| **QSK** | I can hear you between my signals; break in | Can you hear me between your signals? |
| **QSL** | I acknowledge receipt (I got that) | Can you acknowledge receipt? |
| **QSO** | I can communicate with ... (a contact) | Can you communicate with ... ? |
| **QSP** | I will relay the message | Will you relay a message? |
| **QSY** | Change frequency ("I am changing to ...") | Shall I change frequency? |
| **QTC** | I have messages (traffic) for you | How many messages have you? |
| **QTH** | My location is ... | What is your location? |

A few memory hooks: **QRM** is **M**an-made interference and **QRN** is **N**atural noise; **QRS** is **S**low and **QRQ** is the opposite; **QRP** is low power and **QRO** is high.

## How they are used in practice

- **Ask before you transmit.** "QRL?" on CW, or "Is the frequency in use?" on voice, is how you check a frequency before calling CQ. See <Ref to="operating-etiquette" />.
- **One code, both directions.** If someone answers your "QRL?" with "QRL", the frequency is busy. No reply means it is probably clear, but listen and ask again.
- **Stretched meanings.** Hams use some as nouns and verbs: a QSO is a contact, QRP is low-power operating, "QSY" means moving to another frequency, a QSL card confirms a contact. "QRM" and "QRN" are shorthand for human and natural interference.
- **Don't double up.** The code already carries the meaning: "QRP" alone says low power, and "QRZ?" alone asks who is calling.
- **On voice, plain language is clearer.** Q-codes shine on CW, where short is fast. On a repeater, just say what you mean.

<Callout kind="note" title="Not all Q-codes are for hams">Codes beginning with Q are shared among aeronautical, maritime and amateur services, and some mean something different there. In this list the meanings are the common amateur ones.</Callout>
