## What it is

A key is the switch that turns your carrier on and off. All of them do the same job; they differ in **who makes the timing**: your hand, a mechanical pendulum, or an electronic keyer.

| Key | Who times the dits and dahs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight key** | you, for every element | A simple up and down lever. Rugged, and good for learning because you feel each element. |
| **Bug** (semi-automatic key) | a swinging weight makes the dits; you make the dahs | Push one way and a pendulum vibrates, producing a string of evenly timed dits. Dahs are still sent by hand. |
| **Paddles with a keyer** | the keyer | Touch a paddle and the electronics send a perfectly timed element. |

Every individual hand has its own rhythm, called a **fist**. Operators can often recognise a friend's sending.

## Paddles and keyers

A paddle does not make the signal itself. It tells a **keyer**, a small circuit in the radio or a separate box, to generate an element. The keyer makes every dit exactly one unit long and every dah exactly three, with correct gaps, at the speed you set. Your hand supplies only the order.

- **Single paddle** (one lever): push one way for dits, the other for dahs. Simple and common on portable keys.
- **Dual paddle** (two levers, one for dits and one for dahs): pressing both at once, a **squeeze**, makes the keyer alternate dit, dah, dit, dah. This is called **iambic** keying and lets you make letters whose elements alternate, such as C, K and R, with a single squeeze instead of separate taps.

## Iambic A and B

The two iambic modes differ in what happens when you let go during a squeeze.

**Mode A** stops when the element you are on finishes. **Mode B** finishes that element and then sends **one more**, of the opposite kind. Mode B therefore lets you release a little early; it feels natural to many operators who learned on it, while mode A does exactly what the paddles say. Neither is correct; choose the one whose timing suits your hands, and practise with it. Many radios offer both.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Starting out">A straight key teaches rhythm and needs nothing else. Paddles make evenly timed code at higher speeds and many find them easier on the wrist. Many operators move to paddles; some keep a straight key for portable work.</Callout>

- Set the key's contact spacing and tension to feel right; too light invites double dits.
