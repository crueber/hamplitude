## Learn it as sound

Operators do not read dots and dashes. They hear each letter as a rhythm, the way you recognise a word without spelling it. So the goal is a reflex: a sound goes in, a letter comes out, with no counting. Learning from a chart of dits and dahs builds the wrong habit, because you then have to translate, and translation is too slow at speed. For the code itself see <Ref to="morse-alphabet" />.

## Farnsworth: fast characters, long gaps

Speed has two parts: how fast each **character** is sent, and how much total time you get **between** them. Slowing everything down makes each letter sound different from how it will sound at normal speed, so learners then have to unlearn it. The Farnsworth method keeps the **character speed** high (commonly 15 to 20 WPM or more) and stretches only the gaps, so the **effective speed** is low.

<LearningMorse_Farnsworth />

The arithmetic: one PARIS word at an effective speed of s WPM takes 60 ÷ s seconds. The letters themselves take 37.2 ÷ c seconds at character speed c, and the leftover goes into the gaps. As your copy improves, you close the gaps and the effective speed rises toward the character speed.

## Koch: add characters one at a time

The Koch method also starts at full character speed, but with only **two** characters, for example K and M. You copy random groups of just those until you are right about 90% of the time, then a new character is added, and so on. You never meet a slow, wrong-sounding version of a letter, and each new sound is learned against ones you already know. The order varies between courses; the principle is the same.

| | Farnsworth | Koch |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | fast characters, stretched spacing | fast characters, few at a time |
| What grows | effective speed | character set |
| Pitfall | too long a gap invites counting | no early words, so it can feel abstract |

They combine well: Koch for the alphabet, Farnsworth spacing while you speed up.

## Habits that work

- **Short and daily.** Sessions of 15 to 30 minutes usually beat a weekly marathon. Stop before you are tired.
- **Don't look at the chart,** and don't try to name the dits and dahs. Write the letter down, or let it go.
- **Keep going when you miss one.** Dwelling on a missed letter makes you drop the next two.
- **Expect plateaus.** Progress comes in steps, often when you first have to stop writing each letter and start hearing words.
- **Get on the air early.** Many operators will happily slow down for a beginner. See <Ref to="cw-procedures" />.
- **Send, too.** Hearing and sending reinforce one another; see <Ref to="keys-and-paddles" />.

Computers can generate practice text at any speed, and <Ref to="cw-decoding" /> covers when to use a decoder and when it becomes a crutch.
