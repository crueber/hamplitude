## What it is

In FM voice the transmitter's **frequency** is pushed up and down in step with your voice while its power stays constant. It is the everyday mode of VHF and UHF: handhelds, car radios, repeaters and local simplex chats. It sounds clean and quiet, and it is built for short, reliable, local contacts rather than long distances.

## Why it sounds the way it does

Most noise (static, ignition, lightning) changes a signal's **height**, not its frequency. An FM receiver clips off height variations and listens only to frequency, so a decent signal arrives almost noise-free. The price is bandwidth: roughly 10 to 16 kHz, several times an <Ref to="single-sideband">SSB</Ref> signal.

How far the frequency swings is the **deviation**, and it follows how loud you speak. Too much and the signal spills into neighbouring channels and sounds distorted.

<Deviation />

Two behaviours surprise newcomers:

- **Capture.** When two signals share a channel, the receiver locks onto the stronger and the weaker vanishes, rather than both being heard.
- **All or nothing.** Above a threshold the audio is quiet and full. Below it, noise rushes in quickly. FM is poor at digging a weak signal out of the noise; SSB and CW do that far better.

## In practice

- **Speak normally**, a few inches from the microphone. Shouting or pressing the mic to your lips over-deviates.
- **Match the repeater's setting.** Many radios offer a narrow FM setting, roughly half the deviation. A mismatch sounds too quiet or distorted.
- **Set squelch** just above the noise, so the speaker stays silent between transmissions. See <Ref to="squelch-and-tones" />.
- **Use the lowest power that works.** Handhelds often have a high/low switch.
- **Listen first.** Capture means a stronger station will wipe out a conversation you could not hear.
- **Hold the antenna upright**, matching the vertical polarization of nearly everyone else.
