## What it is

WSPR (pronounced "whisper", for Weak Signal Propagation Reporter) is not for conversation at all. A station transmits a tiny beacon with its call sign, location and power, and any station that decodes it uploads a report. The result is a live map of where a given signal is reaching, from almost no power.

## How it works

<Wspr_Beacons />

The signal is extremely narrow and very slow, so a receiver can integrate it over nearly two minutes and pull it out from deep in the noise. Every message is the same shape: who you are, where you are, and how much power you used. Because the transmit power is **in the message**, a listener's report includes both how strong the signal was and how much power produced it.

That makes WSPR a quick answer to "is this band open, and how well does my antenna work?" Leave a low-power beacon running, and see which stations around the world hear it.

## Power in dBm

Power is given in **dBm**, decibels relative to one milliwatt, so a milliwatt-level beacon and a transmitter of a few watts fit the same scale. See <Ref to="decibels" />.

| Power | dBm |
|---|---|
| 1 mW | 0 |
| 10 mW | 10 |
| 100 mW | 20 |
| 1 W | 30 |
| 5 W | 37 |

## In practice

- Both the transmitting and the listening station need accurate computer clocks, since slots start on fixed even minutes.
- A station cannot listen while it transmits, so it alternates slots. Many people run receive-only to study conditions.
- It is one-way: nobody replies, and the "contact" is the report that arrives on the map.
- Use low power and modest audio levels, and treat the transmitter as in constant use during its slot.
