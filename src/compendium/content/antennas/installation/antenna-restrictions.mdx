## What it is

An antenna is a structure on your property, so more than radio rules apply to it. In the US three separate layers can limit what you put up: federal rules, state and local government (zoning, permits, building codes), and private agreements such as a homeowners' association or lease. They work differently, and PRB-1 reaches only the second.

<Callout kind="note" title="This is not legal advice">This article explains the general ideas. Rules differ by place and change over time, and a particular dispute depends on its facts. For a real situation, read your own documents and ordinances, and consult the ARRL, a local club or a lawyer.</Callout>

## Three layers

Federal rules apply everywhere. State and local governments then regulate land use. A private contract can be stricter still, and the law treats it differently.

## PRB-1 in general terms

<G1B_Prb1 />

In 1985 the FCC issued a ruling known as PRB-1, reflected in the Part 97 rules. In general terms it says that **state and local regulation of amateur antennas must reasonably accommodate amateur radio communications, and must be the minimum practical regulation** to achieve a legitimate purpose such as safety or appearance. It does not give you the right to any antenna you like, and it does not exempt you from permits, building codes or reasonable safety rules. A town may regulate, but it must not do so in a way that prevents effective amateur communication. Whether a particular ordinance meets the standard depends on the facts.

## Federal rules for tall structures

<G1B_AntennaHeight />

Separately from local rules, an antenna structure that is more than 200 feet above ground level, or one near a public-use airport, can require notification to the FAA and registration with the FCC. In practice most amateur installations are far below this, but check if you are near an airport.

## Private covenants are different

PRB-1 applies to state and local government regulation. Private restrictions such as HOA covenants and deed restrictions are contracts you agreed to when you bought or rented, and are generally enforced as such. Do not assume they are covered by PRB-1; read them before you buy or build. Congress has at times considered legislation on the subject, so check the current position rather than relying on this article.

## In practice

- **Read first:** your deed, HOA covenants and local ordinances, before you spend money.
- **Ask early.** A friendly request with a clear description, photos or a drawing, and the safety plan often succeeds where a surprise installation fails. Get any approval in writing.
- **Offer a compromise:** a lower or retractable mast, a design that blends in, or a temporary or portable antenna. See <Ref to="stealth-antennas" /> and <Ref to="portable-field-antennas" />.
- **Keep records:** the application, correspondence and what was decided. If you must contest a refusal, a club or the ARRL can advise on how.
- **Safety and codes still apply.** See <Ref to="towers-and-masts" /> and <Ref to="lightning-and-grounding" />.
