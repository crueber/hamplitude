## What it is

A direction-finding (DF) antenna tells you which way a signal is coming from. Take a bearing, move, take another, and the lines cross at the transmitter. Hams use it for fox hunts, for tracking down interference, and for finding a stuck transmitter or an emergency beacon.

## Finding the transmitter

<T8C_Bearings />

One bearing gives a line; two give a point; a third confirms it. Take each from a clear spot, with the antenna held well away from you and metal objects, and aim as accurately as you can. See <Ref to="fox-hunting-and-ardf" /> for the sport and <Ref to="rdf-basics" /> for the technique.

## Peak or null?

<DirectionFindingAntennas_NullVsPeak />

A Yagi or quad shows the strongest signal when it points at the transmitter, but that peak is broad: you can swing it many degrees before the signal level drops noticeably. A small loop does the opposite. It is blind straight through the loop, a **null**, and the signal drops sharply on either side. So a loop gives a much more precise bearing, but only the direction, not how close you are, and it has two nulls 180° apart. You can't tell front from back.

<E9H_LoopPattern />

A **sense antenna** fixes that. A whip or other omnidirectional element, added with the right phase and level, merges with the loop's figure-8 into a **cardioid**, which has a single null. Cardioids are popular for DF because that one null is sharp and has only one direction. A loop usually has an **electrostatic shield**, a metal tube around it with a thin gap, to keep unbalanced capacitive pickup from the surroundings from filling in the null.

## Types of DF antenna

| Antenna | How it finds the bearing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Small loop, with sense antenna | sharp null | simple; shield improves the null |
| Adcock array | two vertical elements, signals subtracted: figure-8 for vertical polarization | does not respond to the horizontal parts of a wave, so it avoids the polarization errors that spoil loops |
| Yagi or quad with an attenuator | peak, sharpened by reducing receiver gain | the usual hunt tool on VHF and UHF |
| Doppler array | switches or spins an antenna and measures the tone-shift in the signal | no moving parts to aim; often built into vehicle systems |

## In practice

- Reflections off buildings and terrain bounce signals in from false directions. A bearing that shifts as you move a few steps is multipath, not the target.
- Near the transmitter the signal can overload the receiver. Turn gain down, or use an attenuator, so the strongest direction stays obvious.
- A signal that arrives by sky-wave comes down from the ionosphere at a steep angle, so a nearby HF transmitter can show no clear direction at all. Hunts are usually done on VHF or UHF, or on ground-wave HF.
- Two bearings from the same place are no use. Move far enough that the lines cross at a sensible angle.
