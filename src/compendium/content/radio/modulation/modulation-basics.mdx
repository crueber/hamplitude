## What it is

Modulation is how information gets onto a radio wave. A transmitter makes a steady **carrier** at the operating frequency, then changes one of its properties in step with the message. A receiver measures that change and recovers the message, which is **demodulation**. Without it, a carrier says only "I am here".

## Three knobs

<ModulationBasics_Chain />

A sine wave has only three properties, so there are only three things to vary. Every mode you will meet is one of them, or a mix:

- **Amplitude**: how strong the wave is. Morse code switches it on and off; <Ref to="amplitude-modulation">AM</Ref> makes it follow the audio.
- **Frequency**: how fast it oscillates. <Ref to="frequency-modulation">FM</Ref> nudges it up and down with the audio.
- **Phase**: where in its cycle the wave starts. Digital modes such as PSK31 flip it; see <Ref to="digital-modulation" />.

## Why a carrier at all

Audio is far too low in frequency to radiate usefully, and every station would land on top of every other. A carrier moves the message up to an assigned frequency, so many stations can share the spectrum, each in its own slice, and so antennas can be a practical size.

## The price: bandwidth

A perfectly steady carrier occupies almost no spectrum, but it carries nothing. Any change you make creates new frequencies either side, the **sidebands**. A faster or bigger change means a wider signal. That is the central trade-off of every mode: information needs bandwidth. See <Ref to="spectrum-and-fourier" /> for why, and <Ref to="bandwidth-and-data-rate" /> for the numbers.

| Mode | What varies | Typical width |
|---|---|---|
| CW (Morse) | amplitude, on and off | under about 200 Hz |
| SSB voice | amplitude and phase, one sideband | 2.4 to 3 kHz |
| AM voice | amplitude | about 6 kHz |
| FM voice | frequency | about 10 to 16 kHz |
| FT8 | frequency, 8 tones | 50 Hz |

Widths are typical, and depend on speed, filtering and settings.

<Callout kind="note" title="Where the work happens">In a receiver, a <Ref to="mixers">mixer</Ref> shifts the signal to a convenient frequency, and a <Ref to="detectors-and-demodulators">detector</Ref> recovers the message. Software-defined radios do the same arithmetic on numbers; see <Ref to="sampling-and-nyquist" />.</Callout>
