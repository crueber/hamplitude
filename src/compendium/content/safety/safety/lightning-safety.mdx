## What it is

An outdoor antenna is a tall, well-connected object reaching into a storm, with a cable running straight into your house. **Nothing makes a station lightning-proof.** Arresters, ground rods and good bonding reduce the odds and the damage, but a direct or nearby strike can still destroy equipment, start a fire or injure people. The most dependable protection is to **disconnect and stay away**.

## People come first

<LightningSafety_FlashToBang />

Lightning can strike miles from the rain. **When thunder roars, go indoors**: a substantial building or a hard-topped vehicle. A tent, an open field, a hilltop or an isolated tree is not shelter. Never be on a tower, mast or roof when a storm is nearby, and do not start a climb if storms are forecast.

Inside, stay clear of anything wired to the outdoors. Do not operate a radio connected to an outside antenna, do not touch feed lines, and avoid corded equipment and plumbing. Surges travel along cables and pipes.

Portable and field operators (Field Day, summit or park activations) should check the forecast beforehand, watch the sky, and pack up early. The time to leave is before the first thunder.

<Callout kind="note" title="If someone is struck">A person hit by lightning does not carry a charge and is safe to touch. Call emergency services and begin CPR if needed.</Callout>

## Disconnecting

<LightningSafety_Disconnect />

Disconnect everything that leads outside: **antenna coax, rotator and control cables, and the AC power cord**, plus any network or data cable. Do it **before the storm arrives**, never once it is overhead. Leave the loose ends clear of the equipment, so a surge on the cable has nowhere to jump to.

A surge protector or power strip is not a substitute for unplugging. A direct strike carries far more energy than any of them is built to handle.

## What protection does

A properly installed system still matters. It places an **arrester** on a grounded panel where each feed line enters, with the ground system **outside** the building and **every ground bonded together**, so no point rises far above the rest during a strike. That diverts much of the surge outside and limits damage. It does not guarantee anything.

See <Ref to="lightning-and-grounding" /> for protecting an installation, and <Ref to="grounding-and-bonding" /> for how the grounds fit together. Follow local electrical code.
