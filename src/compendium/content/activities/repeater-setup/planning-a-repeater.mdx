## Start with the purpose

A repeater is a shared resource that someone must pay for, power and look after for years, so the first question is why. Typical purposes are emergency communications, a club net, linking systems together, covering a specific hole, or acting as a node in a digital voice network. The purpose drives nearly every later choice.

## Do you need a new one?

Spectrum is limited: in a busy area there may be no free channel pair at all, and the frequency coordinator can recommend a different pair or a different band, and may find that nothing suitable is free. A coordinator is a volunteer body that recommends channels and cannot assign them (<Ref to="repeater-frequency-coordination" />). Before spending anything:

- Ask local operators and clubs what they find lacking. A weak spot may be a handheld or antenna problem, not a missing repeater.
- Talk to existing repeater owners. Sometimes an extra receiver or a link to their system fills the gap, and they will have site and landlord experience you can use.
- Ask the coordinator what is typically available in your area, before you buy equipment.

## Band, coverage and cost

Pick the band for the goal: <Ref to="vhf-repeaters" /> for wide coverage and the most existing radios, <Ref to="uhf-repeaters" /> for buildings and smaller antennas, and <Ref to="hf-and-10-meter-repeaters" /> for the rare exception. Coverage is set by antenna height and the handheld at the far end more than by repeater power; see <Ref to="repeater-coverage-and-antennas" />.

Think of the budget in **categories**, not a number:

| Category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Radio gear | receiver, transmitter, controller, duplexer |
| Antenna system | antenna, feedline, mounting, lightning protection |
| Site | rent or a donated space, access, power connection |
| Power backup | batteries, charger, possibly a generator |
| Ongoing | electricity, internet if linked, insurance, spares, travel to the site |

The ongoing costs are the ones people forget. A repeater that costs little to start can cost steadily for years.

## Who owns it, and who takes over

A repeater is an amateur station, so it is licensed like any other. Under 47 CFR 97.5 the FCC grants an individual's station license or a **club station license** (a military recreation station license also exists). A club license is held by a **trustee**: a licensed amateur designated by an officer of a club that has a name, a document of organization, management, a primary purpose in amateur radio and at least four persons. See <Ref to="control-operators" /> and <Ref to="clubs-and-elmers" />.

- **An individual** has the simplest start, but the repeater depends on one person's time, health and address.
- **A club** spreads the work and the cost, and a club can designate a new trustee. It does need members who actually share the job.

Either way, write down the **backup plan** before launch: how the site is powered during an outage, who holds the keys and the control-link codes, and who takes over if the owner moves or stops. See <Ref to="running-a-repeater" /> for succession.
