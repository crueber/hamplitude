## What to compare

Almost all amateur coax is **50 Ω**. What differs is size, flexibility, construction and, above all, **loss**: the power that turns into heat in the cable instead of reaching the antenna. Loss rises with frequency and with length, and falls as the cable gets thicker. Pick the cable for your band and run, not by name.

## Common types

| Cable | Size (approx.) | Dielectric | Loss | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RG-174, RG-316 | about 0.1 in | solid or PTFE | highest | Very short jumpers, portable work |
| RG-58 | about 0.2 in | solid polyethylene | high | Short runs, HF, HT patch cables |
| RG-8X | about 0.24 in | foam | medium | Mobile and short HF runs |
| RG-213 (and RG-8) | about 0.4 in | solid polyethylene | low | HF stations, long-time standard |
| LMR-400, 9913 class | about 0.4 in | foam or air-spaced | lower | VHF/UHF, long runs, and anywhere loss hurts |
| Hardline | rigid or semi-rigid tube | foam or air | lowest | Towers and permanent installations; stiff and harder to terminate |
| RG-59, RG-6 | about 0.25 in | foam or solid | n/a (75 Ω) | TV and video; not matched to 50 Ω ham gear |

"LMR" and "9913" are brand and type names: other makers sell cables of similar construction. Foam or air-spaced dielectric lowers both loss and weight, and gives a higher velocity factor (about 0.8 versus 0.66 for solid polyethylene). See <Ref to="feed-line-loss" />.

## How much power arrives

Approximate loss for common cables, using rounded typical figures (about 5, 3.7, 2 and 1 dB per 100 ft at 100 MHz for the four rows below, scaled by the square root of frequency). Treat the numbers as estimates and use your cable's datasheet for real planning.

| Cable | 7 MHz | 28 MHz | 146 MHz | 446 MHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RG-58 | 1.3 dB | 2.6 dB | 6.0 dB | 10.6 dB |
| RG-8X | 1.0 dB | 2.0 dB | 4.5 dB | 7.8 dB |
| RG-213 | 0.5 dB | 1.1 dB | 2.4 dB | 4.2 dB |
| LMR-400 class | 0.3 dB | 0.5 dB | 1.2 dB | 2.1 dB |

Loss per 100 ft, matched line. A **3 dB** loss halves your power: with 100 W in, about 50 W reaches the antenna and about 50 W warms the cable. 6 dB leaves a quarter.

## Choosing

- **HF, short run:** almost anything works. The loss at 7 to 14 MHz is small, and RG-213 or RG-8X is a comfortable choice.
- **VHF and UHF:** loss is large even over short runs. Use the biggest, lowest-loss cable you can: at 146 MHz, 100 ft of RG-58 wastes three quarters of your power.
- **Power handling** is limited by heating (more loss means more heat) and by the cable's voltage rating. Thin cables and high frequencies come first. The limit falls further with high SWR, because reflections raise current and voltage on the cable. See <Ref to="swr-and-reflections" />.
- **Connectors and flexibility** matter: cable that flexes with a rotator must stay flexible, and every connector must suit the cable.
- **Weather:** use UV-resistant jacket cable outdoors and seal connectors: water ruins a cable. See <Ref to="rf-connectors" />.

<Callout kind="note" title="High SWR adds to the loss">The figures here are for a matched line. When the antenna is mismatched, the loss in the cable goes up, which means a long run of thin cable can hide a bad SWR at the radio.</Callout>
