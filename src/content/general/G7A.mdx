<Concept id="rectifiers" title="Rectifiers: half-wave uses half the cycle, full-wave uses all of it" qs="G7A03 G7A04 G7A05 G7A06 G7A07">

A rectifier turns AC into pulses of DC. A **half-wave** rectifier blocks one half of the AC cycle, so it converts **180°** and needs **one diode**. A **full-wave** rectifier flips the other half too, so it converts **360°** and gives **twice as many pulses**, at twice the AC frequency. It needs **two diodes on a center-tapped transformer**, or four in a **bridge**.

<RectifierTypes />

<Key>Half-wave: 1 diode, 180°. Full-wave: 360°, pulses at twice the AC frequency. Two diodes + center tap = full-wave.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="filter" title="The filter is capacitors and inductors" qs="G7A02">

Rectifier output is lumpy DC. The **filter** smooths it: **capacitors** hold the voltage up between pulses, **inductors** resist changes in current. Diodes and transformers belong earlier in the chain.

<PsuSchematic focus="filter" />

<Key>Power supply filter = capacitors and inductors.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="bleeder" title="A bleeder resistor drains the filter capacitors" qs="G7A01">

Filter capacitors can stay charged for a long time after you unplug a supply. A **bleeder resistor** across the output discharges them when power is removed, so they are less of a shock hazard.

<BleederDecay />

<Key>Bleeder resistor: discharges the filter capacitors when power is removed.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="switchmode" title="Switchmode supplies run fast so the parts can be small" qs="G7A08">

A **switchmode** supply chops the power at high frequency. A transformer or filter part for a higher frequency can be much smaller, so the supply is smaller and lighter than a linear one with a mains-frequency transformer.

<SwitchmodeSize />

<Key>Switchmode: high-frequency operation allows smaller components.</Key>

</Concept>
