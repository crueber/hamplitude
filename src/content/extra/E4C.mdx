<Concept id="noise-floor" title="The noise floor grows with bandwidth and with noise figure" qs="E4C04 E4C05 E4C06 E4C07">

<NoiseFloor />

* **−174 dBm** is the thermal noise in a **1 Hz** bandwidth at the input of a **perfect receiver** at room temperature.
* **Noise figure** = how many dB the noise generated by the receiver is **above that theoretical minimum**.
* **MDS** = **minimum discernible signal**: the weakest signal that stands out of the floor.

<Formula label="Bandwidth raises the floor">10 log(1000 ÷ 50) = 10 log 20 = **13 dB**</Formula>

<Key>Floor = −174 dBm + 10 log(bandwidth) + noise figure.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="selectivity" title="Match bandwidth to the signal, then move the passband off the neighbor" qs="E4C10 E4C12 E4C14">

<Selectivity />

| Control | What it does |
|---|---|
| Choice of bandwidths | **match** the modulation bandwidth: best signal-to-noise, least interference |
| **IF shift** | slides the passband to cut **adjacent-frequency** stations |
| **Narrow roofing filter** | attenuates strong signals **near** the receive frequency: better **blocking dynamic range** |

<Key>Narrow enough to drop the neighbor, wide enough to keep the signal.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="image" title="A high IF pushes the image far from the signal, where a front-end filter can reject it" qs="E4C09 E4C02">

<Image />

The mixer turns **two** frequencies into the IF: the wanted one and its **image**, 2 × IF away. Only a filter **ahead of the mixer** can remove it. A **front-end filter or preselector** also rejects strong **out-of-band** signals. A narrow IF filter acts after the mixer, and a notch filter removes only one frequency.

<Key>Higher IF = image farther away = easier for the front end to reject.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="strong-signals" title="Strong signals overload: attenuate them, filter them or lose to them" qs="E4C08 E4C11 E4C03">

<Attenuator />

On low HF bands **atmospheric noise is generally greater than the receiver's own noise, even after attenuation**. An attenuator cuts the signal and the atmospheric noise equally, and the receiver's own noise is still smaller, so S/N barely moves.

| Strong-signal problem | Where |
|---|---|
| SDR **overload** | input exceeds the A/D converter's **reference voltage** |
| **Capture effect** | FM: a stronger signal on the **same frequency** suppresses the weaker one |

<Key>On low bands, attenuate to stop overload: the noise was outside anyway.</Key>

</Concept>

<Concept id="phase-noise" title="Phase noise on the oscillator rides a strong neighbor onto your signal" qs="E4C01 E4C13">

<Reciprocal />

**Reciprocal mixing**: **local oscillator phase noise mixes with adjacent strong signals** and creates interference to the desired signal. An SDR's master clock with excessive phase noise does the same: it combines with strong nearby signals to generate interference.

<Key>Strong neighbor × noisy oscillator = noise on your frequency.</Key>

</Concept>
